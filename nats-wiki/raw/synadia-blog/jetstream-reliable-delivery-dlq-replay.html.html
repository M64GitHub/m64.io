<p><strong>TL;DR:</strong> JetStream’s delivery guarantees compose from a handful of primitives. A publish acknowledgment confirms an event is persisted in a stream (with <code>Nats-Msg-Id</code> deduplication inside a configurable window). A durable consumer tracks each subscriber’s progress, and explicit acknowledgment gives the consumer three verbs: <strong>ack</strong> (done), <strong>nak</strong> (redeliver immediately, after a delay, or on a backoff schedule), and <strong>term</strong> (stop trying). <code>MaxDeliver</code> bounds redelivery. Any authorized consumer can replay history from a sequence number or a timestamp. NATS has no one-line DLQ config; it has something more composable, and this post builds it in about thirty lines of Go.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>One answer up front, since it is the question most people arrive with: <strong>NATS does not ship a single-config dead-letter queue.</strong> It ships the pieces you assemble one from, and what you end up with is a first-class, replayable stream rather than an opaque holding pen. The rest of this post is the assembly.</p>

<h2 id="how-do-acknowledgments-work-in-nats-jetstream">How do acknowledgments work in NATS JetStream?</h2>
<p>There are two acknowledgments in JetStream, and conflating them is the most common source of confusion: the publish acknowledgment protects the write, and the consumer acknowledgment protects the read.</p>
<p><strong>The publish acknowledgment (<code>PubAck</code>)</strong> is the server’s confirmation that your event has been persisted to the stream according to that stream’s storage and replication configuration. On a replicated stream, the <code>PubAck</code> means the message is committed across the configured replicas, not merely accepted by one node. If you do not receive a <code>PubAck</code>, you do not know the write landed, so you retry.</p>
<p>Retrying safely is where deduplication comes in. Set the <code>Nats-Msg-Id</code> header on publish, and the stream rejects a duplicate with the same ID inside its <a href="https://www.synadia.com/blog/prevent-existing-duplicate-messages-jetstream">deduplication window</a> (configurable per stream, a couple of minutes by default). The <code>PubAck</code> for a duplicate comes back flagged as such, so a retry after a dropped ack is idempotent at the stream boundary.</p>

<p>The precise name for this is <strong>exactly-once <em>publish</em> within the deduplication window.</strong> It does not make end-to-end processing exactly-once; no distributed system can promise that. What you build on top is <em>effectively-once</em> processing: at-least-once delivery combined with idempotent consumers. Hold that thought for the exactly-once question in the FAQ.</p>
<p><strong>The <a href="https://docs.nats.io/learn/jetstream/delivery-and-acknowledgment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">consumer acknowledgment</a></strong> is the read-side counterpart. With an explicit acknowledgment policy (<code>AckExplicitPolicy</code>), the consumer must positively acknowledge each message, and until it does, JetStream considers that message in-flight and owes it redelivery. The consumer’s durable cursor advances only over acknowledged messages, which is exactly why a subscriber that was offline at publish time resumes from its last acknowledged position rather than losing anything.</p>
<p>Two settings govern the in-flight window:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><code>AckWait</code></strong>: how long the server waits for an acknowledgment before it assumes the delivery failed and redelivers.</li>
<li><strong><code>MaxAckPending</code></strong>: the ceiling on unacknowledged messages a consumer may hold at once. This is your backpressure knob; it bounds how much work a slow or wedged consumer can pull without confirming.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="how-do-i-stop-a-poison-message-from-redelivering-forever">How do I stop a poison message from redelivering forever?</h2>
<p>Some messages never succeed no matter how patiently you retry. That is the classic poison message, and without a bound it redelivers forever, starving the consumer of forward progress. <code>MaxDeliver</code> is the bound: after that many delivery attempts, JetStream stops redelivering the message on its own.</p>
<p>But stopping is not enough. A message that silently falls off the end of its redelivery budget is a message you have lost track of. That is where advisories come in. When a message exceeds <code>MaxDeliver</code>, the server publishes an advisory:</p>

So you do the obvious thing and persist them into a stream.</p>
<h2 id="does-nats-have-a-dead-letter-queue">Does NATS have a dead-letter queue?</h2>
<p>No. <strong>NATS has no single-config dead-letter queue.</strong> There is no <code>dead_letter: true</code> field on a consumer. What NATS has instead is the set of primitives you assemble one from, and the DLQ you build is an ordinary JetStream stream with all the retention, filtering, and replay that implies, which beats a special-case holding pen.</p>
<p>The pattern is three parts:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Capture the advisories.</strong> Create a stream that subscribes to the max-deliveries advisory subject. This stream is the index of everything that exhausted its redelivery budget.</li>
<li><strong>Fetch the original message.</strong> Each advisory carries the source stream name and the stream sequence of the failed message. Use a <a href="https://docs.nats.io/learn/jetstream/get-direct" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">direct get by sequence</a> to pull the original payload back out.</li>
<li><strong>Republish to a DLQ subject</strong> captured by its own stream, so the dead-lettered messages live in a durable, queryable place.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="retention-interactions">Retention interactions</h2>
<p>The DLQ pattern touches the source stream by sequence, so how that stream retains messages matters.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><a href="https://docs.nats.io/learn/jetstream/retention-policies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Limits retention</a></strong>: the stream keeps messages until an age, size, or count limit is hit, independent of acknowledgment. This is the friendliest case for the DLQ handler: when the advisory fires, the original message is still there to fetch, as long as your limits are generous enough that it has not aged out between the last delivery attempt and the handler’s direct get.</li>
<li><strong>Work-queue retention</strong>: a message is retained until it is terminally acknowledged, then removed. Here, timing is subtle: the message must still be present when your handler does its direct get. Because exhausting <code>MaxDeliver</code> is what triggers the advisory in the first place, treat the capture handler as time-sensitive and keep it running close to the source, rather than assuming the payload lingers indefinitely.</li>
<li><strong>Interest retention</strong>: a message is retained only while there is interest from consumers that have not yet acknowledged it. As with work-queue retention, the window in which the original payload is available for a direct get is bounded by consumer behavior, so verify the fetch happens promptly.</li>
</ul>
<p>The general rule: on any retention policy that removes messages once they are terminally handled, capture the payload while it still exists. Copying the original into the DLQ stream at capture time, as the handler above does, is what makes the dead-lettered data durable regardless of what the source stream does next.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-i-replay-past-messages-in-jetstream">How do I replay past messages in JetStream?</h2>
<p>Replay is the same read primitive, aimed backward in time. A consumer’s <code>DeliverPolicy</code> sets where it starts:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>All</strong>: from the first message in the stream.</li>
<li><strong>Last</strong>: from the most recent message.</li>
<li><strong>New</strong>: only messages arriving after the consumer is created.</li>
<li><strong>By start sequence</strong>: from a specific stream sequence.</li>
<li><strong>By start time</strong>: from a specific timestamp.</li>
</ul>
<p><code>ReplayPolicy</code> then controls pacing: <strong>instant</strong> delivers as fast as the consumer can take it; <strong>original</strong> reproduces the timing of the original stream, spacing redelivery to match the intervals at which messages first arrived, which is useful for realistic load reproduction.</p>

<h2 id="faq">FAQ</h2>
<p><strong>How do acknowledgments work in NATS JetStream?</strong> There are two. A publish acknowledgment (<code>PubAck</code>) confirms the server persisted your message to the stream per its storage and replication configuration. A consumer acknowledgment confirms a subscriber finished processing a delivered message; under an explicit ack policy the consumer must ack each message, and the durable cursor advances only over acknowledged messages.</p>
<p><strong>How do I retry a failed message with a backoff?</strong> Negatively acknowledge it. <code>Nak</code> schedules an immediate redelivery, <code>NakWithDelay</code> waits a fixed interval, and the consumer’s <code>BackOff</code> schedule applies an escalating series of delays across successive redeliveries. Size <code>MaxDeliver</code> against the backoff schedule so the retries you want all get a chance to run.</p>
<p><strong>Does NATS have a dead-letter queue?</strong> Not as a single configuration flag. You build one by bounding redelivery with <code>MaxDeliver</code>, capturing the resulting max-deliveries advisories into their own stream, and running a small handler that fetches each failed message by sequence and republishes it to a DLQ subject captured by a dedicated stream. The result is a durable, filterable, replayable dead-letter stream.</p>
<p><strong>How do I stop a poison message from redelivering forever?</strong> Set <code>MaxDeliver</code> on the consumer to bound the number of delivery attempts, or call <code>Term</code> in your handler when you detect a permanent failure. Both stop redelivery; <code>MaxDeliver</code> also emits an advisory you can capture for a DLQ.</p>
<p><strong>How do I replay past messages in JetStream?</strong> Create a consumer with a <code>DeliverPolicy</code> of all, by start sequence, or by start time, and choose a <code>ReplayPolicy</code> of instant or original timing. The consumer reads the retained history without involving the original producers.</p>
<p><strong>Is JetStream exactly-once?</strong> JetStream provides exactly-once <em>publish</em> within the deduplication window via the <code>Nats-Msg-Id</code> header, and at-least-once delivery to consumers. End-to-end exactly-once processing is achieved the same way it is in any honest distributed system: build idempotent consumers on top of at-least-once delivery to get effectively-once results. Avoid treating unqualified “exactly-once” as a delivery guarantee.</p>
